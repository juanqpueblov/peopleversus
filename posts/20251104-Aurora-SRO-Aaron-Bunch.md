# Aurora School Resource Officer's Child Exploitation Arrest Exposes a System Built on Blind Trust

Across the U.S., school police officers have been charged with crimes against the very students they were meant to protect. The case of Sgt. Aaron “Jake” Bunch forces hard questions about accountability and the culture of silence in law enforcement.

### A Trusted Officer

**Aurora, CO —** When FBI agents arrested Aurora Police Sergeant Aaron Jacob “Jake” Bunch at his Denver home on October 8, 2025, they didn’t just take one man into custody — they exposed cracks in a system that places police officers inside schools with nearly unchecked access to children and near-total institutional protection.[^1]

For nearly two decades, Bunch was a familiar presence in Aurora’s classrooms. A school resource officer (SRO) and later a supervisor of SROs, he was promoted as part of the solution to school safety. Now he stands accused of exploiting children — the very crime he once lectured teachers and students to identify.

### The Charges

According to the Register of Actions for case 25CR05702, Bunch faces four criminal counts:

1. **Sexual Exploitation of a Child – Sell/Publish** (Class 3 Felony) – C.R.S. 18-6-403(3)(b)  
2. **Sexual Exploitation of a Child – Video** (Class 4 Felony) – C.R.S. 18-6-403(3)(b.5),(5)(b)(II)  
3. **Sexual Exploitation of a Child – Possession** (Class 5 Felony) – C.R.S. 18-6-403(3)(b.5)  
4. **Invasion of Privacy for Sexual Gratification** (Class 1 Misdemeanor) – C.R.S. 18-3-405.6(1),(2)(a)

Court records list the offense date as August 10, 2022. After posting bond on October 17, a motion to limit public access was filed, along with a continuance. The preliminary hearing, originally scheduled for November 5, 2025, is now set for December 3 at 8:30 a.m. in courtroom 3H. The bond was set at $50,000 cash-only.[^2][^3]

As of now, no plea has been entered. A message left with his defense attorney, Harvey Steinberg, by local media was not returned, and Bunch has made no public statements to date.

Despite the gravity of the charges, the Aurora Police Department (APD) has offered little transparency. There has been no public image shared, no dedicated press briefing, and no evident effort to amplify the FBI’s search for victims.

Normally, when APD communicates about an arrest, they share out a mugshot. In Bunch’s case, that mugshot or a department photo is missing from the brief statement issued. This omission is noticeable, especially as the FBI continues to seek victims in the case.

And this isn't the first time. In 2024, another Aurora Police SRO — Officer Egide “DJ” Ndagije — was criminally charged for sending sexual text messages to a 16-year-old student at Aurora Central High School. He later pleaded guilty to official misconduct and was sentenced to probation.[^4] Despite this, APD officials have not raised concerns about patterns of conduct among their officers, prompting questions about the department’s willingness to recognize systemic issues.

### Limited Accountability?

In a brief post on X (formerly Twitter), Chief Todd Chamberlain of APD stated:

> “This is a sad and angering day for the Aurora Police Department and the community we serve. The allegations against this individual are disturbing and completely contrary to the values and mission of this department. This person has brought shame and disgrace to the badge and to an honorable profession. Such behavior undermines the trust and legitimacy that our officers work hard to earn every single day.  
> I want to make it absolutely clear; these actions will never be tolerated. This case will be investigated thoroughly, and the individual will be held accountable through both the criminal justice system and our internal processes.”  
>
> — [@AuroraPD on X](https://x.com/AuroraPD/status/1716633197404358901)[^5]

APD confirmed Bunch was placed on unpaid administrative leave and that an internal investigation is underway — yet no independent review or meaningful accountability for their SRO program has yet been offered.[^6]

Meanwhile, the FBI Denver Division issued a public appeal:

> “Bunch is a law enforcement officer who worked as a School Resource Officer (SRO) and SRO Supervisor in multiple Aurora, Colorado schools. The FBI believes Aaron Jacob ‘Jake’ Bunch may have sought out minor victims while serving in a position of trust, from 2005 to present (October 2025).”  
>
> — [FBI Denver](https://www.fbi.gov/contact-us/field-offices/denver/news/fbi-denver-seeking-potential-victims-of-aaron-jake-bunch)[^1]

That phrasing — “may have sought out minor victims while serving in a position of trust” — highlights what law enforcement rarely confronts: predation can coexist with authority, especially when systems are designed to trust officers rather than scrutinize them.

### A Career Built on Access

Public archives show Bunch joined the Aurora Police Department in 2005 and spent nearly his entire career in roles that gave him close contact with youth.  

He co-presented a seminar titled “Recognizing the Signs of Violent Extremism” following a case involving three Aurora high school students radicalized online.[^7] In 2017, one of his speaking engagements was with the Thornton Citizens Police Academy Alumni Association (TCPAAA). The event page identified him as a coordinator for the Teen Citizens’ Police Academy — a two-week summer program for students ages 14–17 — confirming his mentoring role with minors in educational settings.

The bio confirms that Bunch also served in the Army and holds a bachelor’s degree in criminal justice with a specialization in homeland security.

The FBI form also references the Aurora Explorers, another APD program targeted at young people, ages 14–20, which takes place for 16 weeks, eight hours every Saturday.

These cases show how dangerous it is when officers inside schools operate with minimal oversight: access without accountability becomes the rule, not the exception.

### A System With Little Oversight?

SROs carry authority, enforce discipline, and often mentor children — yet are they subject to the same scrutiny as school staff? They answer to police departments but move freely across school campuses.

As the National Association of School Resource Officers describes its “triad model,” SROs are supposed to serve as educator, counselor, and law enforcement officer:

> “The goals of well-founded SRO programs include providing safe learning environments in our nation’s schools, providing valuable resources to school staff members, fostering positive relationships with youth, developing strategies to resolve problems affecting youth and protecting all students, so that they can reach their fullest potentials. NASRO considers it a best practice to use a ‘triad concept’ to define the three main roles of school resource officers: educator (i.e. guest lecturer), informal counselor/mentor, and law enforcement officer.”  
>
> — [National Association of School Resource Officers](https://www.nasro.org/faq/)

When those charged with protecting children exploit that access, both the police and the schools bear responsibility for granting it so freely and with so few checks.

### The Bigger Picture

This arrest comes amid a national surge in online exploitation. The National Center for Missing & Exploited Children (NCMEC) reported that between January and June 2025, online enticement reports jumped from 292,951 in 2024 to 518,720 in 2025, and reports of AI-generated child sexual abuse material rose from 6,835 to 440,419.[^8]

> “These alarming increases are a wake-up call,” said John Shehan, NCMEC’s senior vice president. “These statistics are not just numbers – they represent children experiencing unthinkable harm.”  
> — Patricia Davis, NCMEC Blog, Sept 4, 2025

### Silence from the School Districts

As of November 4, 2025, neither Aurora Public Schools nor Cherry Creek School District have issued a public statement on their websites about Bunch’s arrest — despite the FBI’s warning that potential victims may include students from those districts. 

### What Comes Next

With the next hearing scheduled for December 3, 2025, the case of Sgt. Aaron “Jake” Bunch continues to unfold quietly — much like so many others. A pending motion to limit public access to court proceedings could make it even harder for the community to learn the truth.

The question isn’t only whether Bunch is guilty. It’s whether Colorado’s policing culture can ever be reformed when police culture appears designed to protect officers, not the public — and certainly not children.
