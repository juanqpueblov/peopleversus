Aurora Police Sergeant’s Child Exploitation Arrest Exposes a System Built on Blind Trust

Across the U.S., school police officers have been charged with crimes against the very students they were meant to protect. The case of Sgt. Aaron “Jake” Bunch forces hard questions about accountability and the culture of silence in law enforcement.

A Trusted Officer, a Familiar Pattern

Aurora, CO — When FBI agents arrested Aurora Police Sergeant Aaron Jacob “Jake” Bunch at his Denver home on October 8, 2025, they didn’t just take one man into custody — they exposed the cracks in a system that places police officers inside schools with unchecked access to children and near-total institutional protection.

For nearly two decades, Bunch was a familiar presence in Aurora’s classrooms. A School Resource Officer (SRO) and later a supervisor of SROs, he was marketed as part of the solution to school violence. Now, he stands accused of child sexual exploitation — the very crime he once lectured teachers and students to recognize.

But despite the gravity of the charges, the Aurora Police Department (APD) has offered little transparency. There was no press conference, no mugshot released, and no dedicated effort to amplify the FBI’s plea for victims. That silence speaks volumes.

The Charges

According to the Register of Actions for case 25CR05702, Bunch faces four criminal counts:

Sexual Exploitation of a Child – Sell/Publish (Class 3 Felony)

Sexual Exploitation of a Child – Video (Class 4 Felony)

Sexual Exploitation of a Child – Possession (Class 5 Felony)

Invasion of Privacy for Sexual Gratification (Class 1 Misdemeanor)

The listed offense date is August 10, 2022. He was booked into the Denver City Jail and later released on a $50,000 cash-only bond. A protection order was issued in October, and his preliminary hearing is scheduled for December 3, 2025.

Police Responses: Controlled Outrage, Limited Accountability

In a brief post on X (formerly Twitter), Chief Todd Chamberlain wrote that the allegations were “disturbing and completely contrary to the values and mission of this department.” He called it “a sad and angering day,” emphasizing that “these actions will never be tolerated.”

Yet, for many in the community, words are not enough. The Aurora Police Department confirmed Bunch was placed on unpaid administrative leave and that an internal investigation is underway — a routine move in such cases. But there has been no public commitment to independent review, despite the fact that Bunch supervised officers across multiple schools in Aurora Public Schools (APS) and Cherry Creek School District.

The FBI Denver Division took the unusual step of appealing directly to the public:

“Bunch is a law enforcement officer who worked as a School Resource Officer (SRO) and SRO Supervisor in multiple Aurora, Colorado schools. The FBI believes Aaron Jacob ‘Jake’ Bunch may have sought out minor victims while serving in a position of trust, from 2005 to present (October 2025).”

That phrasing — “may have sought out minor victims while serving in a position of trust” — underscores exactly what law enforcement often refuses to confront: that predation can coexist with authority, especially when systems are built to shield officers rather than scrutinize them.

A Career Built on Access

Public records show Bunch joined APD in 2005 and spent nearly his entire career in roles giving him close contact with youth.
In 2017, he co-presented a seminar titled “Recognizing the Signs of Violent Extremism” for the Thornton Citizens Police Academy Alumni Association (TCPAAA) — a presentation created after three Aurora high school students were reportedly radicalized online.
He also coordinated the Aurora Teen Citizens Police Academy, a two-week summer program for students ages 14–17, and was referenced in FBI materials as connected to the Aurora Police Explorers, another youth program involving teenagers aged 14–20.

Each of these roles placed him in mentoring or instructional positions over minors — precisely the kind of proximity that, absent strong oversight, can turn from protective to predatory.

Not an Isolated Case: National Failures of Oversight

While Aurora officials downplay the risk of broader harm, this case fits into a national pattern of police officers assigned to schools being charged with crimes against minors.

In Florida (2023), a Broward County SRO was arrested for molesting multiple students on campus after years of complaints ignored by administrators.

In Texas (2024), a veteran SRO from Austin was charged with possession of child sexual abuse material, which investigators later found had been accessed from his patrol laptop.

In California (2022), a Los Angeles Unified SRO was sentenced for coercing explicit photos from teenage girls he met at school events.

A 2019 Associated Press review found that at least 100 law enforcement officers working in U.S. schools had been arrested for sexual misconduct over a five-year period.

The pattern is clear: law enforcement agencies routinely fail to vet, monitor, or discipline their own. When misconduct surfaces, responses are insulated and reactive — not preventive.

Aurora Police are no exception. The department has faced repeated scrutiny for misconduct and racial bias. Yet it continues to expand its footprint in schools under the banner of safety, while accountability mechanisms remain opaque.

A System of Access Without Oversight

Bunch’s case highlights an uncomfortable truth: the structure of SRO programs creates both authority and opportunity.
School officers carry weapons, enforce discipline, and often mentor children — yet are not employees of the district and operate under police department rules, not educational ones.
As Aurora’s own guiding principles state, “SROs are not district employees and should not intervene unless a criminal action has taken place.” In reality, the boundaries are far murkier.

When those tasked with “protecting” children violate that trust, both the police and the schools bear responsibility for allowing such access with minimal checks.

The Bigger Picture: Exploitation on the Rise

The timing of this case is especially troubling given national data showing a surge in online crimes against children.
According to the National Center for Missing \& Exploited Children (NCMEC), reports to the national CyberTipline skyrocketed in early 2025 — with online enticement cases nearly doubling from 292,951 (2024) to 518,720 (2025) in just six months.
Reports of AI-generated child sexual abuse material exploded more than 60-fold, from 6,835 to 440,419.

“These alarming increases are a wake-up call,” said John Shehan, NCMEC’s senior vice president overseeing the Exploited Children Division.
“These statistics are not just numbers – they represent children experiencing unthinkable harm. We need parents, caregivers, educators and communities to stay alert and talk openly with children about online risks.”
(Patricia Davis, NCMEC Blog, Sept. 4, 2025)

The numbers reflect a crisis of scale — one that law enforcement alone cannot solve, especially when the guardians themselves are under investigation.

Silence from Those in Power

As of early November, neither Aurora Public Schools nor Cherry Creek Schools has issued a public statement about Bunch’s arrest. The absence of direct communication has angered many parents, particularly given the FBI’s warning that potential victims may come from within those same districts.

Meanwhile, local police unions and city officials have offered no acknowledgment that systemic reform is needed.
For families, that silence reads as indifference — or worse, self-protection.

What Comes Next

With the next hearing scheduled for December 3, 2025, the case of Sgt. Aaron “Jake” Bunch continues to unfold quietly, in the same opaque way so many others have before it.

The question isn’t only whether Bunch is guilty. It’s whether Colorado’s police culture — and the school systems that welcomed it into classrooms — are capable of confronting what happens when power and proximity replace accountability and transparency.
